I oppose the motion.

**Strict laws regulating AI LLMs would be a mistake because they would be too blunt, too slow, and too easy to weaponize against innovation and free expression.**

First, **LLMs are general-purpose tools**, not a single dangerous product category. They can be used for education, coding, accessibility, research, translation, and creativity. If you impose strict laws on the models themselves, you don’t just regulate misuse—you burden millions of legitimate uses. That means the people who benefit most from AI, including small businesses, students, and startups, are the first to be priced out or locked out.

Second, **rigid regulation will quickly become outdated**. AI changes far faster than legislation. By the time strict rules are written, debated, and enforced, the technology will have moved on. The result is not safety; it is compliance theater—heavy rules that look strong on paper but fail to address real risks. Better to use flexible, outcome-based oversight and targeted enforcement of existing laws against fraud, defamation, cybercrime, and discrimination.

Third, **strict AI laws will entrench big tech**. Large companies can afford legal teams, auditors, and compliance departments. Smaller competitors cannot. So the law would not just “protect the public”; it would consolidate power in the hands of the few firms rich enough to navigate the bureaucracy. Regulation in this space often becomes a moat for incumbents, not a shield for users.

Fourth, **the harms cited are real, but they are better handled by punishing harmful conduct, not restricting the tool itself**. We do not ban printers because people can print lies. We do not heavily regulate word processors because someone might write a scam email. The right approach is to hold bad actors accountable for what they do with AI, while allowing the technology itself to remain broadly accessible.

Finally, **strict laws risk chilling free speech and innovation**. LLMs are fundamentally language systems. When government starts tightly controlling systems that generate text, it opens the door to over-censorship, political abuse, and arbitrary restrictions on legitimate speech.

So the choice is not between “regulation” and “no regulation.” The choice is between **smart, targeted rules that punish harm** and **broad, strict laws that slow progress, protect incumbents, and overreach into ordinary use**. For that reason, I strongly oppose the motion.
